1. Number systems leading to rationals — §1.1–1.2 (pp. 1–3)

What. Natural (counting) numbers N = {1, 2, 3, …} (p. 1, Fig. 1.1 ray). Adding zero (Indian mathematicians’ symbol for “emptiness”; purse-with-₹8-now-empty story, p. 1) gives whole numbers W = {0, 1, 2, …} (p. 2, Fig. 1.2). Subtracting a larger number from a smaller one (6 − 4 = 2 by 4 steps left from 6, but 4 − 6 is impossible in W; Figs. 1.3–1.4) forces negative numbers; together they form integers Z = {…, −2, −1, 0, 1, 2, …} (p. 2). Dividing integers (8 ÷ 5 = 1.6 lies between 1 and 2; (−3) ÷ 4 lies between 0 and −1; −12/5 between −2 and −3) forces numbers-between-numbers: a rational number is a ratio ab, a, b integers, b ≠ 0; the collection is denoted Q (p. 3). Non-negative rationals are fractions, also expressible as decimals and percents (p. 3).

Why. Each extension solves equations the earlier system cannot (x + 8 = 8 needs 0; x + 6 = 4 needs −2; 5x = 8 needs 8/5). Running example (this concept): peeling an orange with 8 carpels — one carpel is the rational number 18 (“Mathematics Alive”, p. 2).

Diagrams (faithful description): Fig. 1.1 — ray starting at 1 with points 1…10 (p. 1). Fig. 1.2 — ray starting at 0 with points 0…9 (p. 2). Fig. 1.3 — number line 0…9 with a dot at 6 (p. 2). Fig. 1.4 — line −8…8 (p. 2). P. 1 also shows the nesting Rational ⊃ Integers ⊃ Natural numbers with sample decimals (0.75, 3.2125, −1.7, 7.66…, 2.1515…, −3, −24, 0, −10, 349, 12, 5). Learning objectives (p. 1): extend fractions to rationals; number line; density; four operations + word problems + ≤3 brackets; properties; squares/roots/cubes/cube roots + estimation; exponents; scientific notation.

Recap Exercise (p. 3): simplest form of 125/200; which is not equivalent to 8/12 among 2/3, 16/24, 32/60, 24/36; bigger of 4/5 or 8/9; add 3/5 + 5/8 + 7/10; simplify 1/8 − (1/6 − 1/4); multiply 234 (rest of Q6 continues p. 4).

2. Rational numbers on the number line; decimal forms — §1.2.1–1.2.2 (pp. 4–5)

2.1 Locating rationals on the line (p. 4)

How. Integers n and −n are equidistant from 0; so are rationals. To plot −34: it is negative (left of 0), between 0 and −1, at 3 of 4 parts — mirror of 34 on the right (Fig. 1.5). −32 = −112 lies between −1 and −2 (p. 4). Fig. 1.6 asks for letters A and B: A = −447 (−327), B = 335 (185) (p. 4). Activity (p. 4): string number line across the classroom; teams fix rational-number cards at the right places; most correct wins.

2.2 Decimal representation (pp. 4–5)

What/How. Divide numerator by denominator: every rational becomes a terminating or non-terminating recurring decimal (p. 4). Note (p. 5): non-terminating non-recurring decimals (π = 3.14159…, √2 = 1.41421…) are not rational (studied in higher classes).

Example 1.1 (p. 5). Decimal forms: (i) 14 = 25/100 = 0.25; (ii) 1320 = 23/20 = 115/100 = 1.15; (iii) −545 = −29/5 = −58/10 = −5.8; (iv) 3/10 = 0.3; (v) 13 = 0.333… (recurring, non-terminating, by actual division).

Try these (p. 5): decimal forms of 4/5, 6/25, 486/1000, 1/9, 314, −235.

3. Signs, equivalents, standard form, comparison — §1.2.3–1.2.6 (pp. 5–9)

3.1 Positive and negative rationals (p. 5)

Rule. Numerator and denominator of the same sign → positive (e.g. 3/11, (−4)/(−6)); of opposite signs → negative (e.g. −3/4, 11/(−6)). 0 is neither positive nor negative. Note: −11/6 = (−11)/6 = 11/(−6) (p. 5).

3.2 Equivalent rationals (p. 6)

Rule. Multiply numerator and denominator by the same non-zero integer (equivalent fractions), e.g. 2/3 = 6/9 and −2/5 = −10/25-type equivalents (p. 6).

3.3 Standard form (p. 6)

What. Denominator positive, HCF(numerator, denominator) = 1, minus sign (if any) only in the numerator (p. 6). How. Method 1: divide successively; Method 2: divide by HCF once.

Example 1.2 (p. 6). (i) 48/(−84): HCF(48,84) = 12 → divide by −12 → −4/7. (ii) (−18)/(−42): HCF = 6 → 3/7 (by −2 then 3, or directly by 6).

Try these (p. 7): (1) equivalent pairs among (−6/4, −18/12), (−4/20, −1/5), (−12/17, −60/85); (2) standard form of −36/96, (−56)/(−72), 27/(−18); (3) mark −2/3, (−8)/(−5), 5/(−4) on the line.

3.4 Comparison (pp. 7–9)

Remember: every positive > 0 > every negative; every positive > every negative; on the line, the right number is greater (p. 7). Four types:

Example 1.3 — Type 1, opposite signs (p. 7). 5/17 vs −10/19: positive wins → 5/17 > −10/19.
Example 1.4 — Type 2, same denominators (p. 8). 1/3 vs 4/3: compare numerators → 1/3 < 4/3.
Example 1.5 — Type 3, different denominators (p. 8). 3/4 vs 5/6: LCM 12 → 9/12 vs 10/12 → 3/4 < 5/6.
Example 1.6 — Type 4, not in standard form (pp. 8–9). (−9)/4-style number vs −2/3: first standardise to −9/4, then LCM 12 → −27/12 vs −8/12; on the line (Fig. 1.7) −8 is right of −27, so −2/3 > −9/4.
Example 1.7 — ordering (p. 9). −3/5, −7/10, −15/20, −14/30, −8/15: standardise, LCM 60 → −36/60, −42/60, −45/60, −28/60, −32/60. Ascending: −15/20 < −7/10 < −3/5 < −8/15 < −14/30 (descending reverses).

4. Rationals between rationals: density — §1.2.7 (pp. 10–12)

What/Why. Between two integers only finitely many (or no) integers lie — but between any two rationals lie infinitely many rationals (p. 10). Running example: the average (midpoint) of orange shares always gives another share between them.

Background figures (pp. 9–10): Fig. 1.8 — integers 4…10 with dark dots on 5–9 (five integers between 4 and 10); Fig. 1.9 — line −5…3 showing no integer between −5 and −4 (p. 9). Method 1 — averages (p. 10, Figs. 1.10–1.11). The average of two numbers lies midway (Fig. 1.10: average of 2 and 8 is 5; Fig. 1.11: ninths-line showing 4/9 between 1/3 and 5/9).

Example 1.8 (p. 10). A rational between 1/3 and 5/9: ((1/3 + 5/9))/2 = (8/9)/2 = 4/9; repeating gives unlimited more.

Think (p. 10): are there rationals between −7/11 and −6/11? (Yes — infinite.)

Method 2 — equivalent denominators (p. 11, Figs. 1.12–1.13). Fig. 1.12 — number line 3/7 = 30/70 … 4/7 = 40/70 with 31/70 … 39/70 between. Textbook illustration (p. 11): 3/7 = 30/70, 4/7 = 40/70 → nine rationals 31/70 … 39/70 between them; zoom again: 37/70 = 370/700, 38/70 = 380/700 → nine more (371/700 … 379/700). Fig. 1.13 shows this with magnifying lenses zooming 0–2 → sevenths → seventieths → seven-hundredths (p. 11).

Example 1.9 (p. 12). Two rationals between −3/4 and −2/5: LCM 20 → −15/20 and −8/20 (Fig. 1.14); e.g. −14/20, −13/20, −12/20, −11/20, −10/20, −9/20 — and ten more exist. Note (p. 12): shortcut — cross-multiply to a common denominator (e.g. −7/11 and −5/9 → range −63…−55 over 99).

Exercise 1.1 (pp. 12–14) — 10 main + 5 objective questions: blanks (integers around −19/5; decimal of −15/4; equidistance of −8/3 and 8/3; next in −15/24, −20/32, −25/40; standard form of −58/78); True/False (0 smallest?; −4/5 left of −3/4?; −19/5 > −15/4?; average lies between?; infinite rationals between 10 and 11?); read points off three number lines; S-Y-N-C-R-A-T-I-O line with CN = NY = YS, RA = AT = TI = IO (find Y, N, A, T, I); plot 9/4, −8/3, −17/5, −15/4; decimals of 1/11, 13/4, −18/7, 12/5, −31/2; five rationals between (−2, 0), (−1/2, 3/5), (1/4, 7/20), (−6/4, −23/10); two rationals between 14/5 and 16/3 by averages; compare (−11/5, −21/8), (−3/4, −1/2), (2/3, 4/5); order (i) −5/12, −11/8, −15/24, −7/9, −12/36 and (ii) −17/10, −7/5, 0/2, −4/19, −19/20; Objectives 11–15 (p. 14): number subtracted from −6/11 to get 8/9; equivalent pair; where −5/4 lies; greatest of −17/24, −13/16, −7/8, −31/32; digit-sum of denominator of 112/528 in simplest form.

5. Operations on rationals; word problems — §1.3–1.4 (pp. 14–20)

All fraction rules govern rationals too (p. 14). Running example (this concept): Sankari’s ribbon — one roll 1834 m; four full rolls plus one-third of a roll (Ex. 1.18, p. 17).

5.1 Addition — Ex. 1.10–1.11 (pp. 14–15)

The book's intro says “four different situations” but names two: Type 1 (same denominators): add numerators over the common denominator; Type 2 (different denominators): standardise, take the LCM, convert to equivalent forms with a common denominator — “so that this reduces to Type 1” (p. 14).

Example 1.10 (p. 14). Add −6/11, 8/11, −12/11 (already standard): (−6 + 8 − 12)/11 = −10/11.
Example 1.11 — Type 2 (p. 15). −5/9 + (−4/3) + 7/12, LCM 36: (−20 − 48 + 21)/36 = −47/36.

5.2 Additive inverse; subtraction — §1.3.2–1.3.3, Ex. 1.12–1.13 (p. 15)

What. The additive inverse of a rational is what added to it gives 0 (4/3 ↔ −4/3). Subtraction = adding the additive inverse. Think: is 0 rational? What is its additive inverse? (0; p. 15).

Example 1.12 (p. 15). Subtract 9/17 from −12/17: −12/17 − 9/17 = −21/17.
Example 1.13 (p. 15). Subtract −2611 from −4522: −93/22 − (−28/11) = −93/22 + 56/22 = −37/22 (= −11522).

5.3 Multiplication; reciprocal; division — §1.3.4–1.3.6, Ex. 1.14–1.15 (pp. 15–16)

How. Multiply numerators × denominators, then standardise. Reciprocal (multiplicative inverse): a/b ↔ b/a since product = 1 (also a ↔ 1/a). Division: multiply the dividend by the reciprocal of the divisor. Note (p. 16): for non-zero b, c, d: (a/b) ÷ c = a/(bc); a ÷ (b/c) = ac/b; (a/b) ÷ (c/d) = ad/bc. Think: multiplicative inverse of 1 and −1 (each is its own: 1, −1). Try these (p. 16): (i) −7/3 by 5; (ii) 5 by −7/3; (iii) −7/3 by 35/6.

Example 1.14 (p. 16). (i) −5/8 × 7/3 = −35/24; (ii) (−6/11) × (−4) = 24/11.
Example 1.15 (p. 16). 7/8 ÷ (−3/4) = 7/8 × (−4/3) = −7/6.

5.4 Word problems — §1.4, Ex. 1.16–1.21 (pp. 16–18)

Example 1.16 (p. 16). Two rationals sum to 4/5; one is 2/15 → other = 4/5 − 2/15 = 10/15 = 2/3.
Example 1.17 (p. 17). Product −2/3; one factor 3/7 → other = −2/3 × 7/3 = −14/9 (multiply both sides by reciprocal 7/3; aliter by cross-multiplication, Fig. 1.15).
Example 1.18 (p. 17). Ribbon: 1834 × 413 = 75/4 × 13/3 = 325/4 = 8114 m.
Example 1.19 (p. 17). 312 + 134 + 238 = 7/2 + 7/4 + 19/8 = 61/8 = 758 (between 7 and 8): subtract 5/8 → 7; add 3/8 → 8.
Example 1.20 (pp. 17–18). Had to multiply by 8/9, instead divided: 9x/8 − 8x/9 = 34 → 17x/72 = 34 → x = 144.
Example 1.21 — three brackets (p. 18). (4/3 − (−3/2)) + ((−5/3) ÷ (30/12)) + ((−12/9) × (−27/16)) = (8/6 + 9/6) + (−1/1 × 4/6) + (3/1 × 3/4) = 17/6 − 4/6 + 9/4 = 13/6 + 9/4 = 26/12 + 27/12 = 53/12.

Exercise 1.2 (pp. 18–20) — blanks (values of −5/12 + 7/15; (−3/6) × (18/−9); (−15/23) ÷ (30/−46); which rational has no reciprocal — 0; inverse of −1); True/False (all rationals have additive inverse?; which equal their own additive inverse — only 0; additive inverse of −11/−17; −1 is its own reciprocal?; does every rational have a multiplicative inverse — no, 0 has none); sums; subtractions; evaluations; divisions; (a + b) ÷ (a − b) for given a, b; simplify-and-show-between-11-and-12; two bracket simplifications; “× 4/3 instead of ÷ 4/3, 70 more” problem; Objectives 11–14 (p. 19): standard form of 3/4 + 5/6 + (−7/12); (3/4 − (5/8 + 1/2))-type values; Objective 15 (p. 20): which have additive inverses (all). Note (p. 20): a/b + c/d ≠ (a + c)/(b + d) — e.g. 3/4 + 1/4 = 1, not 4/8 = 1/2; a statement is true only if 100% true.

6. Properties of rationals — §1.5 (pp. 20–25)

Running example: splitting Sankari’s ribbon bill a × (b + c) — distribute instead of adding first (see distributive illustration).

6.1 Closure (p. 20). a + b and ab are always rational. Textbook illustration (p. 20): a = 3/4, b = −1/2: a + b = 1/4, ab = −3/8 (both rational). Try this: closure of integers holds for subtraction, not division — what about rationals? Verify. (Subtraction stays rational; division fails at ÷ 0.)
6.2 Commutative (pp. 20–22). a + b = b + a; ab = ba. Textbook illustration (pp. 20–22): 7/8 + 3/5 = 35/40 + 24/40 = 59/40 either way; 7/8 × 3/5 = 21/40 either way. P. 21 table: fill properties of integers (closure/comm./assoc./identity/inverse/distributive for + and ×, e.g. 5 + 3 = 3 + 5).

6.3 Associative (p. 22). a + (b + c) = (a + b) + c; a(bc) = (ab)c. Textbook illustration (p. 22): a = −1/2, b = 3/5, c = −7/10 grouping with denominator 10.

6.4 Identity (p. 23). Additive identity 0: a + 0 = a (−3/7 + 0 = −3/7). Multiplicative identity 1: a × 1 = a.

6.5 Inverse (p. 23). Additive: a + (−a) = 0 (a = 11/23 gives 0/23 = 0). Multiplicative: b × 1/b = 1 (b = 17/29).

6.6 Distributive (p. 24). a × (b + c) = a × b + a × c. Textbook illustration (p. 24): a = −7/9, b = 11/18, c = −14/27: b + c = 33/54 − 28/54 = 5/54; a × (b + c) = −35/486 = (a × b) + (a × c).

Exercise 1.3 (pp. 24–25) — fill table for rationals (closure/comm./assoc./identity/inverse/distributive across +, −, ×, ÷); verify laws with given triples; additive/multiplicative inverses; verify distributive instances; verify inverse properties for −7/17 and 17/27; Objectives 7–10 (p. 25): closure fails for division because of 0; (1/2 − 3/4) − 5/6 ≠ 1/2 − (3/4 − 5/6) shows subtraction lacks associativity; inverse-for-addition instance; multiplication distributive over subtraction. Think (p. 25): 1/(1·2) + 1/(2·3) + … pattern → sum of first 7; “same pair, different operations, same answer” exceptions. P. 25 box: why (a/b) + (c/d) ≠ (a + c)/(b + d) revisited.

7. Square numbers — §1.6 (pp. 25–27)

What. 2² = 2 × 2 = 4 (square of side 2), 3² = 9, “4 squared is 16”; n is a square (perfect square) if n = m² for some natural m (49 = 7² yes; 50 no) (p. 26). Table (p. 26): squares 1–20 (1, 4, 9, 16, 25, 36, 49, 64, 81, 100, 121, 144, 169, 196, 225, 256, 289, 324, 361, 400 — extend to 50!).

Ending-digit properties (p. 26): squares end only in 0, 1, 4, 5, 6, 9; ending 1/9 → square ends 1; 2/8 → 4; 3/7 → 9; 4/6 → 6; 5/0 → 5/0; odd² odd, even² even; numbers ending 2, 3, 7, 8 are never perfect squares. Think: is prime² prime? (No.) Are sum/difference/product of two perfect squares always perfect squares? (Verify — product always is.) Try these (p. 26): which of 256, 576, 960, 1025, 4096 are perfect squares; explain why 82, 113, 1972, 2057, 8888, 24353 are not.

Special properties (p. 27): (i) n² (n ≠ 1) is a multiple of 3 or one more; (ii) multiple of 4 or one more; (iii) perfect square ÷ 3 leaves 0/1, never 2; (iv) ÷ 4 leaves 0/1, never 2/3; (v) ÷ 8 leaves 0/1/4, never 2, 3, 5, 6, 7. Notes (p. 27): perfect numbers (6, 28, 496, 8128…) are not squares; a square ending in zero has an even number of zeros (table: 10² = 100 … 2000² = 4000000). Think: “2n non-squares between n² and (n+1)²” — how many between 2500 and 2601?

8. Square roots — §1.7 (pp. 27–36)

What. Inverse of squaring: √n (or n1/2) is the number giving n when multiplied by itself (√81 = 9). Table (p. 27): roots of perfect squares 1–100. Every perfect square has two integral roots (±1 for 1, ±2 for 4, ±3 for 9…); convention: √ denotes the positive root (√4 = 2, not −2) (p. 28). Quick checks (p. 28): 11² = 121 → √121 = 11; 23² = 529 → √529 = 23; 18² = 324 → √324 = 18. Running example: the side of a square field/plot from its area.

8.1 Prime factorisation method (pp. 28–29)

Key idea: each prime occurs in n² twice as often as in n (table: 6, 8, 12, 15 and their squares, p. 28). Steps: factorise → pair identical factors → take one from each pair → multiply.

Example 1.22 (p. 28). √324: 324 = 2 × 2 × 3 × 3 × 3 × 3 = (2 × 3 × 3)² → 18.
Example 1.23 (p. 29). 250 = 5² × 5 × 2 (5, 2 unpaired): × 10 → 2500, √ = 50; ÷ 10 → 25, √ = 5.
Example 1.24 (p. 29). 108 = 2² × 3² × 3 — lone 3 → not a perfect square. Think: smallest factor to ×/÷ 108 into a square?

8.2 Long division method (pp. 30–31)

Textbook illustration 1 (p. 30) — √576 = 24: (1) bar pairs from the right (5̅7̅6̅ → periods “5”, “76”); (2) largest square ≤ first period (2² = 4 ≤ 5) → divisor = quotient = 2; (3) bring down 76 → dividend 176; (4) double quotient (2 → 4_) as new-divisor stem; (5–6) choose digit d with (40 + d) × d ≤ 176: 44 × 4 = 176 (46 × 6 = 276 too big) → remainder 0, quotient 24. Textbook illustration 2 (p. 30–31): √288369 worked stage-by-stage in figures. Running example continues:

Example 1.25 (p. 31). √459684 = 678 (long division).
Example 1.26 (p. 31). Square field 3136 m²: side = √3136 = 56 m; perimeter = 4 × 56 = 224 m.
Try these (p. 32): long-division roots of 400, 1764, 9801.

8.3 Digits; decimals; product/quotient; approximation (pp. 32–35)

Bars = digits (p. 32): number of periods/bars = digits in root (√169 = 13, √441 = 21: 2 bars; √12544 = 112, √27225 = 165: 3 bars). Try these: digits in √14400, √390625, √100000000 (no calculation).

Decimal roots (p. 33) — 5 steps: (1) make decimal places even (append 0 if needed); (2) bar the integral part from the right; (3) bar decimal pairs from the decimal point; (4) long-divide; (5) place the point when the integral part is exhausted (√42.25 = 6.5 shown). Product/quotient rules (p. 33): √(ab) = √a × √b; √(a/b) = √a/√b (b ≠ 0). Fill-in blanks: √36 = √9 × √4-type splits; √(81/…) forms; √144.

9. Cubes and cube roots — §1.8 (pp. 36–39)

What. A cube (perfect cube) is n³ = n × n × n (three identical factors; visualised as 3-D unit-cube blocks). First cubes: 1, 8, 27, 64, 125, 216, … (table p. 36: 1³–4³ with geometric/product/notation columns). Running example (this concept): the “Ramanujan number” 1729 = 12³ + 1³ = 10³ + 9³ — smallest expressible as two cubes in two ways (Hardy’s taxi story, p. 36); also 4104, 13832, 20683.

Properties of cubes (p. 37, table S.No. 1–8): (1) positive³ positive (6³ = 216); (2) negative³ negative ((−7)³ = −343); (3) even³ even (8³ = 512); (4) odd³ odd (9³ = 729); (5) ending 0/1/4/5/6/9 → cube ends same (10³ = 1000, 11³ = 1331, 14³ = 2744, 15³ = 3375, 16³ = 4096, 19³ = 6859); (6) ending 2/8 → cube ends 8/2 (12³ = 1728, 18³ = 5832); (7) ending 3/7 → cube ends 7/3 (13³ = 2197, 17³ = 4913); (8) 1³ + 2³ + … + n³ = (1 + 2 + … + n)² (check for n = 4). Notes: no perfect cube ends in two zeros; a 2-digit cube has 4–6 digits. Try these (p. 37): ones digits of cubes of 12, 27, 38, 53, 71, 84.

Cube root (p. 37). ∛x (or x1/3) cubed gives x (∛27 = 3). Table (p. 37): cube roots 1–16 (∛1 = 1 … ∛4096 = 16).

Example 1.32 (p. 38). 400 = 2×2×2×2×5×5 — only one triplet; needs two more 2’s and one 5 → not a perfect cube.
Example 1.33 (p. 38). 675 = 3×3×3×5×5 — leftover 5×5 → multiply by 5 → 3375 = 3³×5³, a perfect cube (margin ladder: 3|675, 3|225, 3|75, 5|25, 5|1). Think: if “multiplied” → “divided”, how does the solution change?

Prime-factorisation cube root (p. 38) — 3 steps: (1) factorise; (2) group triplets; (3) take one per triplet and multiply.

Example 1.34 (p. 38). ∛27000 = ∛(2³×3³×5³) = 2×3×5 = 30.

10. Exponents and powers — §1.9 (pp. 39–43)

What (p. 39). A power is repeated multiplication: 5² = 25, 5³ = 125. Base 5, exponent 2: the exponent counts uses of the base as a factor. Box quote: positive exponent → multiply; negative exponent → divide. Running example: doubling powers of 2 — 2¹…2¹⁰ = 2…1024, and 2¹⁰⁰ = 1267650600228229401496703205376 (p. 40).

Zero and negative exponents (p. 40). Halving pattern 2⁵ = 32 … 2¹ = 2 forces a⁰ = 1 (a ≠ 0) (same via 3⁵…3⁰); continuing down: 2⁻¹ = 1/2, 2⁻² = 1/4, 2⁻³ = 1/8 → a⁻ᵐ = 1/aᵐ.

11. Standard form & scientific notation; revision — §1.9.5 + end matter (pp. 43–48)

What (p. 43). Standard form = the number as normally written (195 = 1×100 + 9×10 + 5×1). Scientific notation = S × 10ᵃ, 1 ≤ S < 10, a integer: move the decimal until 1–9, attach the 10-power counting places. Positive a → large; negative a → small (p. 44). Note (p. 44): 1.3×10¹²-type (positive exponent) is large; 7.89×10⁻²¹-type (negative exponent) is small.
